Fifty-Six Deals and the Margin That Forced Them

Fifty-six hospital deals in nine months — more than all of 2025. The first quarter alone transacted $14.5 billion of revenue, the most Kaufman Hall has ever recorded. The driver is not ambition. It is a 1.8% margin.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

Hospital merger activity slowed to its lowest level in more than a decade in 2025 as boards waited out federal policy fights. Then it came back all at once. Kaufman Hall counted 22 transactions announced in the first quarter of 2026 — the most in any quarter since early 2020 — followed by 18 in the second quarter and 16 in the third. Fifty-six deals through September, against 46 in all of 2025. The deals are getting larger, the sellers are getting bigger, and a record share of them involve an organization in financial distress. The rebound is not a return to normal. It is a market deciding, deal by deal, which hospitals survive alone and which do not.

The money tells the same story as the count. Total transacted revenue was $18.5 billion in 2025, an eight-year low, down from $39.7 billion in 2024. Transacted revenue in the first quarter of 2026 reached $14.5 billion, the highest quarterly figure Kaufman Hall has recorded since it began tracking the measure in 2018, with the average annual revenue of the smaller party at $657 million, the second highest since 2018. Second-quarter transacted revenue was $7.7 billion, up from $1.4 billion a year earlier, with average seller size of roughly $428 million. Third-quarter sellers brought $8.2 billion in combined annual revenue, with the average seller at $586 million, up from $401 million in the same quarter of 2025. Kaufman Hall counted three megamergers in the first quarter and three in the second, after just two in all of 2025, and two more in the third — deals in which the smaller party has at least $1 billion in annual revenue.

Distress is the thread running through the numbers. Financial distress hit an all-time high in 2025, with 43.5% of transactions involving a distressed party, and the average smaller party in those deals had $345 million in annual revenue — strain that has moved beyond small community hospitals into mid-sized and large systems. Divestitures drove the market into 2026, accounting for 45.6% of announced transactions in 2025 and more than two-thirds in the first quarter. For-profit systems were the seller in 11 deals in 2025 but the acquirer in only one. Three of the 16 third-quarter sellers were distressed, and five of the quarter’s deals were divestitures.

The margins explain why. Kaufman Hall’s National Hospital Flash Report, drawing on data from more than 1,300 hospitals, showed the operating margin index at 1.8% in August, down from 3.8% in July and the lowest since February; year to date it stood at 2.5%. About 43% of hospitals are operating at a loss, compared with about 33% before the pandemic. Non-labor expenses, including supplies and drugs, rose 6% per adjusted discharge through August. Premier estimates hospitals will lose $68.6 billion in revenue across 2026 and 2027 from rising uninsurance. Roughly 720 rural hospitals are at risk of closure, about one-third of all rural facilities, according to the Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform. A hospital losing money at a 1.8% industry margin does not have the luxury of waiting out the policy cycle.

The deals defining 2026 are not distressed rescues alone. The largest pending transaction is Sutter Health’s plan to acquire Allina Health, a combination that would create a $26 billion nonprofit with 39 hospitals, more than 400 care sites, 18,000 physicians, and 88,000 employees across California, Minnesota, and Wisconsin. In October, Rush University System for Health and Endeavor Health announced plans to merge into a 12-hospital Chicago-area system with nearly $11 billion in revenue, Endeavor having pledged a $1 billion investment, with closing expected in early 2027. HealthPartners announced a deal to acquire Essentia Health, a combined organization with more than $12 billion in revenue. Prisma Health and Erlanger Health System are combining with about $2 billion of investment. Risant Health agreed to acquire INTEGRIS Health in Oklahoma, its third hospital system after Geisinger and Cone Health. Cain Brothers counted 23 deals announced in the third quarter, with volume through three quarters outpacing the same period in 2025 by 57%.

The honest version is that both readings of the surge are true. “We’re witnessing proactive positioning over reactive consolidation,” Kris Blohm, managing director and co-leader of Kaufman Hall’s M&A practice, said in the firm’s second-quarter report — boards choosing partners earlier and on purpose, in a year when operating performance stayed tight. It is also true that the broader healthcare deal market cooled in the third quarter: total healthcare M&A was $66.5 billion, down 48% from the second quarter’s $128.5 billion, though up 17% from the third quarter of 2025. Hospitals are consolidating faster while sponsor-led physician roll-ups slow sharply. And the policy overhang has not lifted: the enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies expired at the end of 2025, and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act’s effects begin in 2027.

That is what a hospital is worth in 2026: the value of the alternative. Public hospital companies trade at roughly 10 times earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization on a long-run average — HCA at 8.5 times, Community Health Systems at 7.5 times, Ardent at 6.1 times as of June 30, 2026 — but a sale is not priced off a public multiple. It is priced off the cost of staying independent at a 1.8% margin with 43% of the industry losing money, against the value of a partner that can keep the doors open. The fifty-six deals are fifty-six boards doing that arithmetic and reaching the same answer.
