How Long a Hospital Sale Takes, from Board Vote to Closing

A hospital sale takes a year if everything goes right. The certificate of need alone can add six months — and skipping the workbook is the most common reason deals miss their date.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The board voted in January to explore a sale. The letter of intent was signed in April. The definitive agreement was signed in September. The deal closed the following June — seventeen months after the board vote, eleven months after the LOI, and four months after the date in the agreement. The delay was not a negotiation problem. It was a certificate of need application that took 240 days to review, a state attorney general review that ran past its deadline, and a Medicare change-of-ownership filing that could not be submitted until both were done. Nobody on the board had budgeted for any of it, because nobody on the board had been told that the regulatory clock starts the day the board votes, not the day the agreement is signed. That is the first thing to understand about how long a hospital sale takes: the timeline is set by the regulators, and the regulators do not work on the deal's schedule.

The regulatory layer is what makes a hospital sale different from any other business sale, and it is what sets the timeline. A hospital deal carries a Certificate of Need approval, Anti-Kickback Statute exposure, Stark Law physician arrangements, HIPAA business associate transfers, Joint Commission accreditation continuity, and Medicare provider number reassignment — and a deal template that ignores any of those layers will stall at signing or trigger a regulatory clawback months after close. The Certificate of Need is the largest single item: 35 states plus the District of Columbia require CON approval for a change of ownership at an acute care hospital, and the review windows run 60 to 270 days depending on the state. Tennessee, North Carolina, Georgia, and New York have active programs with windows in that range. California, Texas, and Colorado have repealed CON or never had it, and require only routine licensure transfer. A CON exemption letter is preferable where the state allows it, since exemption review runs 30 to 60 days against as much as 270 days for a full CON plus appeal. Skipping the CON workbook — the pre-signing map of the target's licensed bed count, services, and county service area against the state's bed-need formula — is the single most common reason hospital deals miss their target close date. A delayed CON adds 90 to 180 days, and during that period the deal is burning cash and the seller's leverage is falling.

The other approvals stack on top of the CON rather than replacing it. A Hart-Scott-Rodino premerger notification is required if the transaction crosses the $119.5 million threshold set by the FTC for 2025. Roughly one in four hospital deals required state attorney general or Federal Trade Commission review before clearing, according to Kaiser Family Foundation 2025 hospital transaction tracking. A CMS Form 855A change-of-ownership filing through PECOS, the Provider Enrollment Chain and Ownership System, governs whether the Medicare provider number transfers or terminates at close — and it cannot be filed until the state approvals are in hand. Some states add their own clocks: Connecticut requires the attorney general and the public health commissioner to each decide within 120 days of receiving a completed application, with public hearings in the primary service area; Nebraska runs a 60-day department review and a 60-day attorney general review under a single unified process when a CON is also required; Tennessee requires a notarized change-of-ownership application and letter of intent 60 days before the anticipated closing date. Each of those clocks starts on a different date, and none of them can be shortened by paying more.

The deal volume behind the timeline is running at its highest level in years, which makes the regulatory capacity question sharper. Kaufman Hall counted 22 hospital transactions announced in the first quarter of 2026 — the highest first-quarter total in six years — with $14.5 billion in transacted revenue, the most the firm has ever recorded for a first quarter. The second quarter added 18 deals and $7.7 billion in transacted revenue, against $1.4 billion in the second quarter of 2025, the lowest since 2018. The third quarter added 16 deals and $8.2 billion. That brings the 2026 total to 56 announced transactions, against 46 in all of 2025 — the lowest annual count since Kaufman Hall began tracking hospital transactions in 2011. Six mega-mergers, deals where the smaller party has more than $1 billion in annual revenue, have been announced so far in 2026, already surpassing the total for all of 2025. The average seller ran $428 million in annual revenue in the second quarter and $586 million in the third. Among the largest deals of the third quarter, Chattanooga-based Erlanger Health System, a $1.6 billion organization, signed a nonbinding letter of intent to join Greenville-based Prisma Health. The volume means the state agencies reviewing these deals are busier than they have been in a decade — and a busy agency is a slow agency.

Inside the regulatory clock runs a second timeline that the agreement has to carry: diligence. A hospital deal's diligence is not a financial review with a healthcare label. It is a Stark Law review of every physician arrangement, an Anti-Kickback Statute review of every referral relationship, a HIPAA review of every business associate agreement, a Joint Commission accreditation continuity plan, and a Medicare provider number analysis — and each of those can produce a finding that changes the price or the structure. A physician arrangement that is not at fair market value is not a footnote; it is a Stark violation that the buyer inherits at closing. A referral relationship that runs afoul of the Anti-Kickback Statute is not a diligence item; it is a federal exposure. The diligence period is where those findings surface, and the purchase agreement has to be drafted to survive them — with representations, indemnities, and escrows that reflect the fact that the buyer is acquiring a regulated entity, not just a building with beds in it.

The counterargument is that the long timeline is a feature, not a bug. A hospital is not a clinic and not a factory: it holds a license, a Medicare provider number, physician arrangements governed by Stark and the Anti-Kickback Statute, and a community's emergency care. The review exists because the last owner of a hospital that was sold too fast is usually a headline. The other counterargument is that the timeline is compressing for the sellers who plan for it: two-thirds of the second-quarter transactions involved independent systems proactively seeking partners rather than distressed sellers, and a proactive seller can run the CON workbook, the attorney general pre-filing, and the Hart-Scott-Rodino analysis before the LOI is signed — which is the only way to make a 12-month timeline real. The deals that take 17 months are usually the ones where the regulatory work started after the agreement was signed.

The practical move is to file the CON workbook before the LOI, not after the definitive agreement. Map the licensed beds, the services — cardiac cath lab, obstetrics, oncology, organ transplant — and the county service area against the state's bed-need formula while the board is still deciding whether to sell. Run the attorney general pre-filing and the Hart-Scott-Rodino threshold analysis in parallel with diligence, not after it. And write the purchase agreement's outside date around the regulatory calendar, not the negotiation calendar: a 12-month outside date with a 6-month extension for regulatory approval is honest in a CON state, and a 9-month outside date in a non-CON state is honest only if the Medicare change-of-ownership filing is ready to go on the day the agreement is signed. A hospital sale that takes a year is not a slow sale. It is a sale that was planned on the regulators' calendar — and the regulators' calendar is the only one that counts.
