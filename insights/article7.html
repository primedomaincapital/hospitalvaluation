The Real Estate Inside the Deal: What the Hospital's Land and Buildings Are Worth Separately

The hospital sells for one price. The land, the medical office building, and the lease sell for another — and the real estate is sometimes worth more than the operations.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The hospital deal was priced at $42 million, and the buyer and seller spent six months arguing about the operations — the payer contracts, the physician arrangements, the quality scores. The real estate was an afterthought, a line in the schedules. Then the appraiser valued the land and the medical office building separately, and the number came back at $18 million — more than 40% of the total deal value, sitting in a line item nobody had negotiated. The seller's attorney asked the obvious question: if the real estate is worth $18 million on its own, why is it being sold as part of a $42 million operating deal? The answer was that nobody had priced it separately, and the price of the whole was less than the sum of its parts. That is the most expensive oversight in hospital transactions, and it happens because the real estate is valued by cap rates while the hospital is valued by earnings — and the two numbers rarely move together.

The medical office market sets the real estate price, and in 2026 it is a seller's market for the right buildings. CBRE reported a record average asking rent of $25.40 per square foot for medical outpatient buildings in the first quarter of 2026, up 1.6% from a year earlier, and expects average rent to reach a new high this year as construction completions fall to their lowest level in a decade. Medical outpatient investment volume rose 78% in the first quarter of 2026 to $2.9 billion, and the average cap rate fell to 6.9% — a price of roughly 14.5 times annual rent. The pricing is strongest with a lease term longer than seven years and a creditworthy tenant, both of which an established hospital or practice can provide. The worked example from the practice real estate guides makes the math concrete: a practice needs 10,000 square feet, leases it at $25.00 per square foot on a triple-net basis — $250,000 in the first year, with 3% annual escalators — or buys an equivalent building for $3.6 million, a 6.9% cap rate on that rent and in line with the national average. The purchase is financed with $360,000 down, a $1.8 million bank loan at 7.5%, and a $1.44 million SBA loan at the September 2026 rate of 6.54%, both amortized over 25 years. The numbers are illustrative, but the relationship is not: at a 6.9% cap rate, every dollar of rent is worth about $14.50 of building value.

The sale-leaseback is the mechanism that separates the two values, and the market for it is deep. In a sale-leaseback, a practice or hospital that owns its building sells the property to an investor and signs a long-term lease to stay in place, turning real estate equity into cash for expansion, equipment, new locations, a partner buyout, or retirement planning, while operations continue without interruption. The investor gets a long-term healthcare tenant, and healthcare tenants typically sign initial leases of 7 to 15 years with annual rent increases, with triple-net terms passing taxes, insurance, and most maintenance to the tenant. Asking cap rates for single-tenant healthcare net-lease properties run from about 5% to 9%, with most between 5.5% and 7%, depending on tenant credit, lease term, and location. The examples from 2026 show the range: a Patient First urgent care portfolio trades at about 5.5% to 6.5% cap rates, with centers typically selling for $4 million to $8 million on 15-to-20-year leases with 10% rent increases every five years; a Temple, Texas medical office sold at a 7.00% cap rate for $4.83 million on a nine-year triple-net lease running through 2035; an Aurora Health and ThedaCare two-property portfolio in Oshkosh, Wisconsin was offered at $13.5 million at a 6.75% cap rate, at just over $251 per square foot — below estimated replacement cost — with Aurora paying about $13 per square foot against market rents of about $21, roughly 62% of potential rental upside on future negotiations.

The separation matters most in the hospital deal itself, because the buyer and the seller usually want different things from the real estate. When the practice itself is sold, buyers often prefer to lease the real estate rather than buy it, which leaves the physicians with a separate asset and a market lease — and the physicians with a rent check that continues after the operating sale is done. A cash-out refinance produces liquidity without a sale at all. The SBA 504 program finances owner-occupied commercial real estate including medical offices, with the SBA portion capped at $5.5 million for most projects and a 25-year rate of 6.54% at the September 2026 pricing, up from 5.72% in March. The 2025 tax law made 100% bonus depreciation permanent for property acquired after January 19, 2025, which changes the after-tax math of owning versus leasing in a way that did not exist two years ago. And medical office occupancy runs well above most commercial property types, with hospital-adjacent buildings in many markets effectively full — which is why the cap rates have compressed even as interest rates have stayed high.

The counterargument is that separating the real estate raises the operating cost, and the rent is a fixed cost that does not flex with volume. A hospital that sells its building and leases it back at above-market rent has converted an asset into a liability: the rent is due whether the beds are full or not, and a 15-year triple-net lease at a rent set to maximize the sale price can sink the operating company it was meant to fund. Sale-leaseback rents can be set high, and the buyer of the real estate is underwriting the lease, not the hospital — which means the rent has to be compared to local market rents for similar medical buildings, not to the rent that makes the sale price work. The other counterargument is that the separation creates a second negotiation with a second counterparty, and in a hospital deal the real estate negotiation can delay the operating deal by months. Both objections are right, and both point to the same discipline: price the real estate on market rent and market cap rates, not on the rent that makes the total deal price look good.

The practical move in a hospital transaction is to price the dirt separately, in writing, before the operating price is agreed. Get an independent appraisal of the land, the medical office building, and any separate parcels, on market rent and the prevailing cap rate for the tenant's credit and lease term. Decide before the LOI whether the real estate is included, leased back, or carved out — because the decision changes the operating price, the financing, and the tax treatment, and it is far harder to change after the agreement is signed. A hospital's land and buildings are worth what the medical office market says they are worth, and the medical office market in 2026 says they are worth more than they have ever been. The operating business is worth what its earnings say it is worth. When the two numbers are priced together, one of them is usually wrong — and it is usually the real estate, which is the number nobody argued about.
